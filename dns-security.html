DNS Security & Privacy

Learn how DNS answers can be attacked and how security tools help.

Three ways DNS can be attacked

DNS spoofing

An attacker sends a fake DNS answer that points a name to the wrong address. This could send someone to a fake website.

Cache poisoning

A resolver stores a false answer in its cache. Other people who ask the same question may get that answer until it expires or is removed.

DNS hijacking

An attacker changes DNS settings or records. For example, someone who gets into a router or domain account could change where DNS requests or website traffic go.

Read more: Cloudflare: DNS cache poisoning

DNSSEC: checking the data

DNS Security Extensions, called DNSSEC, use digital signatures to help check DNS answers. A validating resolver follows a chain of trust to check where signed data came from and whether it was changed. DNSSEC can also check signed answers saying that a name or record does not exist.

DNSSEC does not hide your DNS questions or guarantee that a website is safe. It needs correct signing and validation to work. Unsigned data does not get the same protection.

Read more: RFC 4033

DoH and DoT: protecting the connection

Data validation and transport encryption solve different problems

DNSSECChecks the authenticity and integrity of signed DNS data.Does not hide the question.

DNS over TLS (DoT)Uses TLS to protect DNS transport, normally on port 853.Protects that connection, not every later DNS hop.

DoH and DoT can reduce eavesdropping and tampering between a client and its resolver. They do not automatically provide DNSSEC validation or make browsing anonymous. A resolver's logging policies still matter.

Practical habits

  • Keep routers, operating systems, and DNS software updated.
  • Protect domain-management accounts with strong passwords and multifactor authentication.
  • Use a trustworthy resolver and review its privacy policy.
  • Use encrypted DNS where supported and allowed by your network's policies.
  • For domains you manage, coordinate DNSSEC setup with your DNS provider and registrar.
  • Pay attention to HTTPS certificate warnings even when DNS appears to work.
